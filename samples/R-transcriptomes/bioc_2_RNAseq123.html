Introduction

Today, we continue to use the ‘RNASeq123’ package to analyze an old mouse project (the GSE63310 dataset that the RNAseq123 vignette uses).

The Vignette demonstrates the important of a process called “normalization”, but in fact this dataset does not really need it. The authors created artificial variation in a couple of samples to demonstrate their point. So… we’ll skip this today, but you should always take a look at the sample distributions in new analyses. This is a task much better suited for R than for Excel.

Another task well-suited for R is unsupervised clustering. I agree with the Vignette authors that this is one of THE MOST IMPORTANT exploratory analyses that we perform. It tells us whether replicates of our sample cluster near one another and away from other sample types, thereby providing some observable distinction about the phenotype of interest. If the samples don’t separate from each other, it will be hard to find reliable signals that tell us about the underlying biology.

Some sections from the previous notebooks is re-printed below; skip to the “Unsupervised Clustering” section to pick up the new material.

Data Transformation

Gene expression is rarely considered at the level of raw counts because 1) Samples sequenced at a greater depth will result in higher counts and 2) Longer genes will have greater counts.

In this analysis, we use CPM and log-CPM values because they can be calculated using a counts matrix alone without more external resources.

#raw counts are converted to CPM and log-CPM values using the cpm functions in edgeR 
cpm <- cpm(x)
lcpm <- cpm(x, log=TRUE)

Remove genes with no counts, or spurious counts

All datasets will include a mix of genes that are expressed and those that are not expressed; 19% of genes in this dataset have zero counts across all nine samples.

#How many genes have no counts in ALL 9 samples?
tb <- table(rowSums(x$counts==0)==9)

#calculate percent non-expressed genes 
tb["TRUE"]/(tb["FALSE"]+tb["TRUE"])

#Keep only "well-expressed" genes, rather than only non-zero gene counts.
keep.exprs <- filterByExpr(x, group=group)

Unsupervised clustering Plot

An unsupervised cluster plot can be made in with the limma package using the plotMDS() function. Limma is a package that was designed to work with older technology (microarrays), but is statistically sound with new sequencing technology even today, and it remains the most popular tool for various RNA-Seq analyses.

The plot we generate below separates samples based on the leading-fold-change that best represents the largest proportion of variation in the data (regardless of whether that fold change separates sample replicates or not - it is unsupervised after all). In this dataset, we hope that whatever causes the most difference between any two samples will position replicates near each other, and different cell types far away, but we don’t bias the output by informing the software of our preference. (There ARE supervised clustering approaches where we might guide selections based on our prior knowledge of the dataset)

The first dimension (the x-axis) explains the most variation among samples, and subsequent dimensions having smaller and smaller effects. There are infinite dimensions (welcome to the multi-verse!), but plotting dimensions higher than 5 or 6 comes with diminishing returns and is rarely done. If samples cluster by a given factor in any of these dimensions, it suggests that the factor contributes to expression differences and is worth including in mathematical models (typically linear modelling). We will skip this part!

In this dataset, samples cluster by experimental group (cell type) over dimension 1 and 2. The first dimension explains the largest proportion of variation in the data, so this matches our expectation and is good news! Further, the largest transcriptional difference is observed between basal and both luminal cell types over dimension 1. We therefore expect a greater number of differentially expressed genes for comparisons involving basal samples, and relatively small numbers when comparing ML to LP. Datasets where samples do not cluster by experimental group may show little or no evidence of differential expression in the downstream analysis.

#To create the MDS plots, we assign different colours to the factors of interest. Dimensions 1 and 2 are examined using the color grouping defined by cell types.
col.group <- group
levels(col.group) <-  brewer.pal(nlevels(col.group), "Set1")
col.group <- as.character(col.group)
plotMDS(lcpm, labels=group, col=col.group)

Differentially Expressed Genes

For a quick look at differential expression (DE) levels, the number of significantly up- and down-regulated genes can be summarised in a table.

As usual, the significance is defined using an adjusted p-value cutoff that is set at 5% by default.

Some studies require more than an adjusted p-value cut-off. For a stricter definition on significance, one may require smaller p-values (for example, something called a Bonferroni correction), or log-fold-changes (log-FCs) to be above a minimum value.

Notice, the larger numbers of DE genes observed for comparisons involving the basal population are consistent with our observations from the MDS plots.

Also notice these numbers don’t quite match the Vignette. That’s because we (I) decided to ignore the small batch effect observed from which lane the samples ran on from the sequencing machine.

#DE genes with a basic p-value cut-off 
summary(decideTests(efit))

#DE genes with stricter fold change cut-offs 
tfit <- treat(vfit, lfc=1)
dt <- decideTests(tfit)
